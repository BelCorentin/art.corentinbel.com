The sky is a weather forecast written in water. Here are the ten cloud genera and a few special ones, painted at the heights where they usually live, from the sea to the top of the weather. Tap any cloud to read what it is, how it forms, and what it means if you fly a paraglider, sail, or ride a wing foil. Then run a summer day and watch a cumulus grow.

A day of thermals

The land heats, thermals rise, and cumulus appear where they cool to their dew point. Whether they stay small or become a storm depends on the air above.

Three habits worth keeping

Measure the base

Cumulus bases sit where a rising thermal reaches saturation. A parcel of dry air cools by about 10 °C per km; its dew point falls by only about 2 °C per km. The gap closes at roughly 8 °C per km, which gives the rule of thumb base ≈ 125 m × (T − Td) rule of thumb. Pilots often quote it as 400 ft per °C. It works for surface-based thermals on a sunny day, not for layer clouds.

Watch the tops, not the bases

A sky of flat-topped cumulus with wide gaps is a good thermal day. Towers that keep getting taller and harder-edged by late morning or early afternoon are the warning rule of thumb. Turrets on mid-level altocumulus in the morning (castellanus) say the same thing even earlier.

Read the sequence

Cirrus, then a milky cirrostratus with a halo, then grey altostratus, then nimbostratus and steady rain: the textbook arrival of a warm front. The order is reliable; the timing is not. Rain often follows the first thickening veil within roughly 12 to 24 h rule of thumb, and many cirrus skies bring nothing at all.
