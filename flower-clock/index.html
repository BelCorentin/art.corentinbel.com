The flower clock

In 1751 Carl Linnaeus proposed a garden that tells the time: plant flowers that open and shut at known hours, and read the hour from which ones are open. Here is that garden, set to your clock.

i.How to read the garden

The dial runs once around in 24 hours: midnight at the bottom, noon at the top, morning on the left. Each flower's stem is planted at the hour it opens. Tap a flower to see its hours, drag the slider to move the day, or press play and watch the garden wake up from the left and fall asleep on the right. The little window in the middle is the sky over Uppsala, with its cathedral's twin spires.

Try guess: the hand and the hour disappear, and you read the time from the flowers alone, as Linnaeus hoped a gardener could.

ii.Linnaeus's list

In Philosophia Botanica (1751) Linnaeus gave a horologium florae, a flower clock: a list of plants with the hours at which their flowers opened and closed, as observed in Uppsala, at about 60° north. Most of the entries are dandelion relatives (goat's-beard, chicory, hawkweeds, sow-thistles, cat's-ears), whose flower heads open and shut day after day. For some plants he recorded only an opening time, for others only a closing time.

He also sorted the flowers that move each day into three kinds. Only one kind is any use for a clock.

meteorici

Open and close with the weather: later, or not at all, when the air is damp, cloudy or cold.

tropici

Open in the morning and close before evening, but the hours drift through the season as the days lengthen and shorten.

aequinoctiales

Open and close at fixed hours, whatever the day. These are the clock flowers.

The times on this page are his where he gave them, and marked otherwise where they are not. Read them all as "about". He kept local solar time in a northern summer; your clock, your latitude and your weather will move them, often by an hour or more.

iii.Why a flower keeps hours

The movement. A petal opens when its inner face grows or swells more than its outer face, so it bends outward; it closes when the balance reverses. In flowers that open and close many times, like the dandelion family and water lilies, this is done by repeated growth or by changes in water pressure in the petal cells. Many other flowers open once and never close again (van Doorn & van Meeteren 2003).

The cues. Light and temperature trigger many of these movements: tulips and crocuses open in a warm room and shut in the cold. But in a number of species the rhythm carries on for a while in constant light or darkness, which shows that an internal circadian clock is doing part of the timing. In petunia, a core clock gene (LHY) holds back scent production until evening, when the hawkmoths that pollinate it fly (Fenske et al. 2015).

The name. Daily opening and closing of flowers and leaves is called nyctinasty, from the Greek for night and pressing together. The same family of movements folds the leaflets of beans, clovers and mimosa at dusk.

The reasons. The usual explanations are timing and protection: be open when your pollinators are about (bees in the morning, moths at dusk) and keep pollen dry and warm at night or in rain. These are plausible and partly tested, but they differ from species to species.

iv.Why it never quite worked

A clock that stops on cloudy days, runs fast in the south, and is only fully planted for a few weeks a year.

Weather shifts the hours, which Linnaeus knew: that is his meteorici. Day length shifts them across the season. Latitude shifts them too, so a list made in Uppsala is wrong in Paris or Fréjus. Worst of all, the plants on the list do not all flower in the same months, so the full garden never exists at once. Linnaeus may never have planted one. Several botanical gardens tried in the nineteenth century, with mixed results.

Still, the idea stuck, because it is true in spirit. A meadow does run on a schedule. Go out at seven and at three and you will see a different set of flowers.

v.The mechanical cousin

Today "floral clock" usually means something else: a real clock movement whose hands sweep over a flower bed planted as a dial. Edinburgh's in Princes Street Gardens (1903) is often called the first. Geneva's L'horloge fleurie, in the Jardin Anglais by the lake, was installed in 1955 and replanted with the seasons ever since. They keep perfect time, and the flowers in them do nothing at all. Linnaeus's version is the opposite: no machinery, and the flowers are the hands.

vii.Three I love

None of these is on Linnaeus's list, but each keeps, or refuses, hours of its own. The mimosa below follows the clock at the top of the page.

Mimosa

Acacia dealbata, the winter gold of the Tanneron hills above the Côte d'Azur. Its feathery leaves fold their leaflets together at night, a sleep movement of the same kind that shuts the flowers.

Sweet violet

Viola odorata opens its scented flowers in early spring. Later it makes small flowers that never open at all and pollinate themselves inside the bud (cleistogamy): flowers that ignore the clock entirely.

Damask rose

Rosa × damascena is the main rose grown for rose oil, around Kazanlak in Bulgaria and Isparta in Turkey. Its flowers are picked by hand early in the morning, which growers say gives the richest oil.
