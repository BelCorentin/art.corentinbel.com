the raga clock

In Hindustani classical music, ragas have hours. Bhairav belongs to dawn, Yaman to the first dark, Malkauns to the dead of night. The wheel below is one day; it shows where we are right now and what a musician would traditionally play. Tap a raga to read its scale and hear it over a tanpura.

How to read the wheel

The circle is one day. Noon sits at the top, midnight at the bottom, sunrise on the left and sunset on the right. The outer ring is the colour of the sky at each hour. The band inside it splits the day into eight prahars of about three hours: four of daylight counted from roughly 6 am, four of night from roughly 6 pm. The plaques mark where each raga is traditionally placed. Drag the sun, or the slider, to walk through the day.

Treat each plaque as "about this time, give or take an hour". Books and gharanas (schools) disagree in the details, and some prahar charts start an hour later. The broad shape is shared, and that is what the wheel shows.

Reading the notes

Indian solfège names seven notes relative to a movable tonic, Sa. Sa and Pa never change. Re, Ga, Dha and Ni can be lowered (komal); Ma can be raised (tivra). That makes the same twelve semitones as a piano, named from wherever Sa happens to be.

The aroha and avaroha are not just a scale going up and down. They are the conventional path, with its skipped notes and its little turns back. A raga's real identity lives in its characteristic phrases, its ornaments and which notes it rests on. The vadi is the most emphasised note, the samvadi the second, usually a fourth or a fifth away. What you will hear here is a skeleton. A sitarist would never play it this plainly.

Is the time theory real?

It is a real tradition, not a law of nature. Linking ragas to hours and seasons is old and widely respected among Hindustani musicians, and many concerts still follow it. But the specific rules you read in textbooks were largely written down in the early 20th century by V. N. Bhatkhande, who sorted hundreds of ragas into ten parent scales (thaats) and looked for patterns. Three of the rules of thumb associated with his system:

  • Twilight ragas. At dawn and dusk, the sandhi prakash ("joining of light") hours, the classic ragas use komal Re with shuddh Ga and Ni: Bhairav and Lalit in the morning, Marwa and Puriya Dhanashree in the evening. They are the moments the wheel's colours change fastest.
  • Ma as the signpost. Shuddh Ma tends to dominate in the morning, tivra Ma in the evening. Compare Bhairav (dawn) with Marwa or Yaman (dusk). Todi, a morning raga with tivra Ma, is the famous counterexample.
  • Lower half, upper half. Ragas whose vadi sits in the lower half of the octave (Sa to Pa) tend to fall between noon and midnight; those with the vadi in the upper half (Ma to high Sa) between midnight and noon. Yaman (vadi Ga, evening) and Bhairav (vadi Dha, morning) fit neatly.

Plenty of ragas bend these rules, and scholars such as Jairazbhoy treat them as generalisations drawn after the fact from a living repertoire rather than a cause. Nothing in acoustics or physiology makes a scale belong to the morning. It is better understood as a shared language of moods tuned to the light, which musicians and listeners learn together.

Two more honest footnotes. Some ragas belong to a season instead of an hour: the Malhar family to the monsoon, Basant and Bahar to spring. And South Indian (Carnatic) music has its own raga system, built on 72 parent scales called melakartas; it knows a few time associations but does not organise performance around the clock the way the North does.

What you are hearing

The tanpura is the drone behind all of it. Four strings are plucked in a slow loop, usually Pa in the lower octave, Sa, Sa, then the low Sa. For ragas that drop Pa, the first string is retuned, usually to Ma (as here for Lalit and Malkauns) or to Ni (here for Marwa). Its shimmering sound comes from the jawari, a broad curved bridge that the vibrating string grazes. That contact keeps pumping energy into high harmonics, so the tone blooms after the pluck instead of only dying away. C. V. Raman described this in 1921. Here it is imitated by letting each upper harmonic swell a little later than the one below it.

The plucked notes use the Karplus–Strong algorithm (1983): a short burst of noise circulates in a delay line whose length sets the pitch, and every lap gently averages neighbouring samples, which damps the high frequencies the way a real string loses its brightness. Letting the delay length wobble with the string's amplitude adds a faint buzz. With meend on, each note glides from the previous one instead of being struck fresh, the way a sitarist pulls the string sideways across a fret. Notes marked with andolan sway slightly below their pitch.
